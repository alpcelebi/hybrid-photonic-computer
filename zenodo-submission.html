Hybrid Photonic Computer Architecture: An Electronic Host and a Photonic Linear-Algebra Accelerator Coupled by Asynchronous Priority Queues

Abstract

This paper describes a conceptual hybrid computer in which a conventional binary electronic host offloads linear-algebra workloads (matrix–vector products, convolutions) to a photonic accelerator. The two sides communicate through asynchronous submission and completion queues with three priority classes, held in electronic SRAM, and the accelerator is preempted at tile boundaries so that no optical memory is required.

Rather than encoding 256 optical intensity levels per symbol, which would require roughly 50 dB of signal-to-noise ratio, operands are split into base-4 digits and transmitted as PAM-4 symbols, a format already in production in optical Ethernet. Results are recombined electronically by shift-and-add, using the identity x = Σ 4ᵏ·xₖ. A parametric energy model, with every assumption stated, puts the photonic core at roughly 100–150 fJ per 8-bit MAC in its best configurations. That is about 5–7× below a contemporary GPU's ~700 fJ/MAC and far short of the 100× often quoted. The model also shows that thermo-optic weight tuning without wavelength multiplexing erases the advantage entirely. Because matrix multiplication is only part of total facility energy, the system-level reduction is bounded at roughly 40% under the assumptions used. Version 1.0 made several claims that this version withdraws or corrects (Appendix A).

Keywords: photonic computing, hybrid electronic–photonic architecture, AI accelerator, PAM-4, digit slicing, thin-film lithium niobate, wavelength division multiplexing, priority queues, preemption, energy per MAC

1. Introduction

Data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5% of global electricity consumption, and demand is growing quickly with AI workloads [15]. Much of the arithmetic in neural-network inference is matrix multiplication, an operation that optical interference can, in principle, perform with an energy that scales favourably with vector size [1, 5, 9].

In practice, photonic accelerators are limited not by the speed of light but by everything around it: the digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital converters at the boundary, the optical signal-to-noise ratio that bounds precision, the power needed to hold weights in tunable elements, and the memory traffic that feeds the accelerator [1, 5]. This paper proposes a system organisation that takes those limits as the starting point. Its contributions are:

  1. A system-level arrangement: an unmodified binary electronic host, priority-classed asynchronous queues, and tile-granular preemption, so that the accelerator never needs optical memory (Sections 3 and 7).
  2. Base-4 digit slicing, which lowers the optical precision requirement to PAM-4 at the cost of extra passes and makes the number base a design parameter (Section 6).
  3. A parametric energy-per-MAC model that identifies which component dominates and bounds the facility-level benefit (Section 8).

No novelty is claimed for any individual component. Each is drawn from published work, cited where it is used.

2. Related Work

Photonic neural-network hardware. Shen et al. demonstrated matrix multiplication with meshes of Mach–Zehnder interferometers [2]. Feldmann et al. used phase-change-material weights and a frequency comb to run parallel convolutions across many wavelengths [4]. Shastri et al. review the field [1], and Pérez-López et al. describe programmable, self-configuring photonic circuits [11]. Ahmed et al. reported an electronic–photonic processor that runs ResNet and BERT models with near-electronic precision for many workloads [6]. That system is the closest prior art to the organisation proposed here.

Energy analyses. Nahmias et al. compared photonic and electronic multiply–accumulate hardware and found the photonic advantage concentrated at large vector sizes and low precision (≈4 bits or less) [5]. Miller analysed the energy limits of optoelectronic conversion [9]. Both results motivate the digit slicing of Section 6 and the model of Section 8.

Materials and devices. Thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) modulators operate at CMOS-compatible voltages [3, 7]. Silicon nitride offers low-loss waveguides [8]. Multi-level signalling is mature in optical communications [10].

Multi-valued logic and queues. Multi-valued electronic logic has been studied for decades without displacing binary logic in processors [12]. Host–accelerator command queues with doorbells, completion interrupts and priority arbitration are standard. NVMe, for example, specifies weighted round-robin arbitration with an urgent priority class [13]. The queue design in Section 7 adopts those mechanisms.

3. Architecture Overview

The system has three parts. An electronic host runs the operating system and applications. A control die holds the queues, the converters, the scheduler and the partial-sum memory. A photonic core performs linear operations only. All state lives in electronics. The photonic core is stateless between passes.

4. Electronic Host

4.1 Why the Host Stays Binary

Version 1.0 proposed base-4 logic with levels at 0, 1.1, 2.2 and 3.3 V. Two problems rule this out. First, at a fixed supply voltage VDD, a binary signal has a gap of VDD between levels while a four-level signal has VDD/3, so the noise margin is worse, not better. Second, dynamic power scales as αCV²f. Raising the supply to 3.3 V from a modern ~0.75 V costs about (3.3/0.75)² ≈ 19× in dynamic power.

Same supply, level gap: binary = VDD quaternary = VDD/3
Dynamic power ratio: (3.3 V / 0.75 V)² ≈ 19×
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Decision: host logic is standard binary CMOS (~0.7–0.9 V)

A binary host also means existing operating systems, compilers and applications run unchanged. The accelerator needs only a driver, a runtime and a compiler back-end, as any GPU or NPU does.

4.2 Where Multi-Level Encoding Belongs

Multi-level encoding works where the physical medium is analog and the extra levels are paid for by slower or more careful sensing. Two examples are storage cells (MLC, TLC and QLC NAND flash, already standard) and optical links (PAM-4). The design uses multi-level encoding in exactly those two places, not in logic gates [12].

5. Photonic Accelerator

5.1 Scope

The photonic core computes linear operations y = Wx, which covers matrix–vector products, convolutions and fixed linear transforms. Non-linear activations, normalisation, control flow and all storage stay in electronics. The design targets inference. Training requires FP16/BF16-class precision for gradients and weight updates, which is beyond what the optical path provides, so training is out of scope.

5.2 Precision and Signal-to-Noise Ratio

Each additional bit per optical symbol costs about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio. The ideal quantisation limit is SNR ≈ 6.02·b + 1.76 dB:

Table 1. Ideal SNR required per optical symbol as a function of bits per symbol.
Bits per symbolLevelsRequired SNR (ideal)Practicality

8256 (v1.0 proposal)49.9 dBNot practical with intensity detection

Shot noise, laser relative intensity noise and receiver thermal noise all eat into these margins. Intensity is non-negative, so signed values need differential (balanced) detection or offset encoding.

5.3 Speed

Throughput is set by the symbol rate of modulators, converters and detectors, not by the propagation speed of light. Electrical signals in copper also travel at a large fraction of c. TFLN modulators reach tens of GHz of electro-optic bandwidth at CMOS-compatible drive voltages [3, 7]. Time of flight through a millimetre-scale mesh is in the picosecond range, so the latency of a single pass is low, but sustained throughput is symbol rate × parallelism. The model assumes f = 10 GBd per channel, with parallelism from an N×N mesh and M wavelengths.

6. Base-4 Digit Slicing

6.1 What 4⁴ = 256 Does and Does Not Give

Version 1.0 presented 4⁴ = 256 as a property unique to base 4 that makes the electronic–photonic bridge lossless. It is not unique: 2⁸ = 16² = 256 as well, and an 8-bit byte already maps bijectively onto 0–255. Any power-of-two base gives the same exact mapping. The real cost of the bridge is analog conversion (DAC and ADC), which is independent of how the digital bits are grouped.

6.2 Where Base 4 Actually Helps

The identity does become useful when it decides how many optical levels each pass must resolve. Write an 8-bit input as four base-4 digits and use linearity:

x = Σₖ 4ᵏ·xₖ , xₖ ∈ {0,1,2,3} ⟹ Wx = Σₖ 4ᵏ·(W xₖ)
Each pass W·xₖ needs only PAM-4 inputs (13.8 dB instead of 49.9 dB).
The control die recombines the four partial results by shift-and-add.

The cost is four passes per 8-bit operand, or four parallel wavelength groups. The number base thus becomes a design parameter that trades precision against throughput:

Table 3. Digit-slicing options for an 8-bit operand.

Base 4 is chosen because PAM-4 transmitters and receivers are already in volume production for optical interconnects, and because it halves the pass count of bit-serial (base-2) operation.

6.3 The Remaining Bottleneck: Output Precision

Slicing the inputs does not reduce the precision needed at the output. Exactly accumulating N = 64 products of 2-bit digits and 4-bit weights needs log₂(64·3·15) ≈ 11.5 bits. ADCs in the energy class assumed in Section 8 deliver about 6 bits at 10 GS/s, so partial sums are quantised. Quantisation-aware training can tolerate this for inference. Otherwise N per tile must shrink, which raises the conversion energy per MAC. This trade-off is the central open problem of the design.

7. Asynchronous Priority Queues

7.1 Where the Queue Lives

Version 1.0 placed the queue in a LiNbO₃ crystal. The Pockels effect changes the refractive index only while the field is applied, so LiNbO₃ modulates but does not store data. In this version the queues are SRAM ring buffers on the control die (or in host memory, NVMe-style), with doorbell registers for submission and completion queues for results. LiNbO₃ moves to where it excels: the input modulators and, optionally, the electro-optic weight elements.

7.2 Priority Classes and Arbitration

Table 4. Priority classes. Arbitration follows the urgent-class + weighted-round-robin scheme of [13].

P2 BackgroundBatch analytics, offline evaluationGuaranteed minimum share (e.g. 1 in 16 slots) plus aging, so it never starves

7.3 Tile-Granular Preemption Without Optical Memory

A photonic pass holds no state once it completes. Tile indices, partial sums and digit counters all live in control-die SRAM. The scheduler can therefore switch to a P0 job between any two tiles at negligible cost. The real preemption latency comes from weights: if the P0 job needs different weights, the mesh must be reprogrammed. That takes nanoseconds with electro-optic elements but microseconds or more with thermo-optic ones. Latency-critical work should therefore have its weights kept resident on a dedicated mesh or wavelength group.

7.4 The Non-Blocking Contract, Stated Precisely

Submission never blocks. If a queue is full, the call returns BUSY (backpressure) and the host decides whether to retry or yield. Completion is signalled by an interrupt, with coalescing under load. Asynchrony raises throughput by overlapping independent requests, such as batched or pipelined inference. It cannot remove data dependencies: autoregressive decoding, for example, must wait for each token before it can submit the next.

submission_entry {
  priority : P0 | P1 | P2
  weight_id : handle to resident weights
  input_ptr : host/SRAM address of operand tile
  output_ptr : address for result tile
  shape : (rows, cols, batch)
  digits : passes per operand (e.g. 4 for 8-bit, base-4)
  timestamp : submission time (for aging)
  cq_id : completion queue to post to
}

8. Energy Model

Consider an N×N weight mesh driven at symbol rate f with M wavelengths. Each pass performs M·N² multiply–accumulates, needs M·N input conversions and M·N output conversions, and burns laser power PL per wavelength plus static tuning power PT for the whole mesh. With D passes per operand (digit slicing), the energy per full-precision MAC is:

EMAC = D · [ (EDAC + EADC) / N + PL / (f·N²) + PT / (f·M·N²) ]
Conversion cost amortises as 1/N. Laser cost amortises as 1/N². Tuning cost is additionally amortised across wavelengths.

Three conclusions follow:

  1. Weight-element technology decides whether there is any advantage. Thermo-optic tuning on a single wavelength (A) is worse than the GPU. Either WDM, which amortises tuning (B), or electro-optic / non-volatile weights (C, D) are needed.
  2. The best case is about 5–7×, not 100×. Once tuning is solved, conversion dominates. Further gains need larger N, which ADC precision and optical loss limit, or cheaper ADCs.
  3. Throughput per core is modest. At 10–164 TMAC/s per core against ~990 TMAC/s for a GPU, many cores per package are needed, so area and packaging become first-order problems.

These numbers are favourable to photonics. They exclude memory traffic, the host and control-die logic, while the GPU figure includes on-chip data movement.

8.1 Facility-Level Bound

If a fraction φ of facility energy goes to the linear algebra that can be offloaded, and that part becomes S times cheaper, Amdahl's law [16] bounds the total reduction:

Reduction = 1 − [ (1 − φ) + φ / S ] φ = 0.5, S = 7 ⟹ ≈ 43%
Memory, networking, host CPUs and facility overheads are unchanged.

Cooling water scales roughly with the heat rejected, so it obeys a similar bound, not the 90% reduction claimed in v1.0. For context, Li et al. estimate that a GPT-3-class model consumes about 500 ml of water per roughly 10–50 medium-length responses, depending on location and season [14].

10. Space Applications (Speculative)

Two properties may be useful on spacecraft. First, dielectric waveguides do not suffer the charge-deposition upsets that flip bits in transistor storage. Second, a lower energy cost for linear algebra helps power-limited missions. Both are partial advantages only. Photodetectors, lasers, converters, the control die and the host are still semiconductor electronics and need radiation hardening. Optical materials also suffer radiation-induced attenuation, and lasers need thermal stabilisation. Version 1.0's claims of radiation immunity, a 5,000× capability gain and terabit deep-space links are withdrawn. Deep-space optical links are limited by received photons (link budget), not by the number of wavelength channels. Any space use would require radiation testing of the full optoelectronic stack.

11. Limitations and Open Problems

  1. Output precision. ~6-bit ADCs quantise the partial sums (Section 6.3). Larger tiles reduce energy per MAC but need more ADC bits.
  2. Weight reprogramming. Thermo-optic elements are slow and power-hungry. Electro-optic elements are large. Phase-change elements have limited endurance. Weight-stationary operation with batching is assumed.
  3. Optical loss scaling. Insertion loss grows with mesh depth, which raises the laser power needed for a given SNR.
  4. Integration and packaging. The photonic core, the control die and the lasers must be co-packaged with dense electrical and optical I/O.
  5. Lasers. Wall-plug efficiency of 10–30% and temperature sensitivity add heat and require control loops.
  6. Memory wall. The accelerator does not reduce weight or activation traffic from host memory, which dominates many inference workloads.
  7. Training. Out of scope at the available precision.
  8. Software. A compiler back-end must map models onto tiles, digit slices and resident weights. The host software stack is unchanged.
  9. No validation yet. The model in Section 8 has not been checked against device-level simulation or measurements.

12. Conclusion

This revision recasts the hybrid photonic computer as a binary electronic host with a photonic linear-algebra accelerator, coupled by priority-classed asynchronous queues and preempted at tile boundaries so that no optical memory is needed. The 4⁴ = 256 relation survives as a design choice: base-4 digit slicing lets each optical pass use mature PAM-4 signalling.

The energy model gives a sober outlook. A best-case core-level gain of about 5–7× over a contemporary GPU is plausible. That gain depends on electro-optic or non-volatile weights, or on wavelength multiplexing, and it shrinks at the facility level because of Amdahl's law. The most valuable next steps are, first, replacing the placeholder parameters of Table 5 with measured device data and, second, a circuit-level study of the ADC precision–tile size trade-off.
